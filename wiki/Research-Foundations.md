# Research Foundations

Rung is a **practical synthesis**, not a claim that one teaching method is universally best.

The system combines several mechanisms that have independent support in learning research, while also using design ideas adapted from MAPS_Lean. Those two kinds of support should not be confused.

This page explains what the research supports, what it does **not** establish, and how Rung uses the finding.

---

## 1. Retrieval practice

### What the research says

Roediger and Karpicke (2006) compared repeated study with repeated retrieval of prose material. Repeated study produced stronger very-short-delay performance, but retrieval produced substantially better retention on delayed tests.

Primary source:

- Henry L. Roediger III & Jeffrey D. Karpicke (2006), **Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention**, *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249–255. [DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)

### Rung use

When knowledge must be remembered, ask the learner to retrieve it rather than immediately displaying it again.

Examples:

- "What was our diagnostic question?"
- "List the failure modes from memory."
- "Explain the rule without looking."

### Limit

Retrieval practice is not evidence that every lesson should become a quiz or that factual recall alone constitutes mastery. Rung combines retrieval with application, explanation, and transfer.

## 2. Self-explanation

### What the research says

Chi and colleagues found that stronger learners generated more self-explanations while studying worked examples, connecting example steps with underlying principles and developing more example-independent knowledge.

Primary source:

- Michelene T. H. Chi, Miriam Bassok, Matthew W. Lewis, Peter Reimann & Robert Glaser (1989), **Self-Explanations: How Students Study and Use Examples in Learning to Solve Problems**, *Cognitive Science*, 13(2), 145–182. [DOI: 10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1)

A later experiment explicitly prompted students to self-explain an expository science text and found greater gains in understanding than a rereading control condition.

Primary source:

- Michelene T. H. Chi, Nicholas de Leeuw, Mei-Hung Chiu & Christian LaVancher (1994), **Eliciting Self-Explanations Improves Understanding**, *Cognitive Science*, 18(3), 439–477. [DOI: 10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3)

### Rung use

Do not ask only for an answer. Ask the learner to explain:

- why the method applies;
- what evidence matters;
- why an alternative fails;
- what changed their mind;
- how they would recognize the same structure again.

### Limit

Not every verbal explanation is good evidence. Learners can produce fluent but incorrect explanations. Rung therefore requires explanation **plus verification**.

## 3. Guidance, cognitive load, and worked examples

### What the research says

Cognitive-load research emphasizes that working memory is limited and that instruction can be designed to reduce unnecessary load while helping learners build usable knowledge structures.

A concise modern overview:

- Fred Paas & Jeroen J. G. van Merriënboer (2020), **Cognitive-Load Theory: Methods to Manage Working Memory Load in the Learning of Complex Tasks**, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 29(4), 394–398. [DOI: 10.1177/0963721420922183](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721420922183)

Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark (2006) argued that minimally guided instruction is often less effective and efficient for novices than instruction with substantial guidance, with the advantage of external guidance decreasing as learners acquire enough prior knowledge to provide more internal guidance.

Primary source:

- Paul A. Kirschner, John Sweller & Richard E. Clark (2006), **Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work**, *Educational Psychologist*, 41(2), 75–86. [DOI: 10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1)

### Rung use

This is a major reason Rung rejects a rigid "never tell the learner anything" version of Socratic teaching.

If the learner lacks prerequisite knowledge, the teacher should explain the minimum necessary concept or provide a worked analogous example, then return the reasoning to the learner.

See [The Assistance Ladder](Assistance-Ladder).

### Limit

"Guidance is useful" does not mean "always show the full procedure first." The useful amount and form of guidance depend on prior knowledge, task complexity, and the kind of learning desired.

## 4. Expertise reversal and guidance fading

### What the research says

Instructional techniques that benefit novices can lose effectiveness—and sometimes become detrimental—as learner expertise increases.

Primary source:

- Slava Kalyuga, Paul Ayres, Paul Chandler & John Sweller (2003), **The Expertise Reversal Effect**, *Educational Psychologist*, 38(1), 23–31. [DOI: 10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4)

### Rung use

The teacher should deliberately **fade support**.

```text
explanation / worked example
→ structured cues
→ diagnostic questions
→ independent attempt
→ audit only
```

The assistance level should respond to demonstrated competence rather than remain fixed because the teacher has one preferred style.

### Limit

Rung's eight-level assistance ladder is a system design, not a validated eight-stage psychological scale. The research supports the broader principle that instructional support should be adapted to learner expertise.

## 5. Productive failure

### What the research says

Kapur (2014) reported two randomized controlled studies in mathematics in which students who attempted problem solving before instruction achieved stronger conceptual understanding and transfer than students taught the concepts and procedures first, while both approaches produced high procedural knowledge.

Primary source:

- Manu Kapur (2014), **Productive Failure in Learning Math**, *Cognitive Science*, 38(5), 1008–1022. [DOI: 10.1111/cogs.12107](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12107)

### Rung use

Rung normally gives the learner a meaningful attempt before unnecessary explanation. An attempt can expose prior knowledge, misconceptions, and strategy choices, and can give later explanation something concrete to reorganize.

### Limit

This finding should not be generalized into "failure is always good" or "instruction should always come second."

Productive failure depends on task design, learner preparation, and subsequent consolidation. Random guessing, missing prerequisites, or prolonged confusion are not automatically productive.

Rung therefore includes a **productive-struggle test** and moves up the Assistance Ladder when struggle stops generating useful reasoning.

---

## 6. Transfer as a stronger criterion than repetition

Rung treats transfer as central because the goal is not to reproduce the teacher's exact example but to use the underlying principle in a changed situation.

The sources above provide several relevant signals:

- Chi et al. (1989) explicitly discuss **example-independent knowledge** arising from stronger self-explanation.
- Kapur (2014) reports differences in ability to transfer to novel problems.
- Expertise and cognitive-load research emphasizes building knowledge structures that can guide future performance rather than merely surviving one instructional episode.

### Rung use

A mastery test should change the surface form enough that the learner has to recognize the underlying structure.

See [Mastery and Transfer](Mastery-and-Transfer).

### Limit

"Transfer" is not one single ability. Near transfer and far transfer differ substantially, and success depends on domain knowledge. Rung therefore defines transfer relative to the actual target skill instead of treating it as a universal binary property.

# What is Rung's own synthesis?

The following are useful **design choices**, but they should not be presented as if a study validated Rung as a complete package:

- the exact eight-level Assistance Ladder;
- the specific error taxonomy used in [Diagnosing Mistakes](Diagnosing-Mistakes);
- the `NEEDS_BASELINE → GUIDED → PRACTICING → TRANSFER_TEST → INDEPENDENT` status model;
- the exact one-question-at-a-time rule;
- adapting MAPS evidence labels to demonstrated learner knowledge;
- the particular format for mistake ledgers and standing principles;
- the combination of MAPS project-governance ideas with the learning mechanisms above.

These are reasoned system-design choices intended to make the process inspectable and consistent.

The right standard is therefore:

> **Use research to constrain and inform the system, test the system in practice, preserve uncertainty, and revise rules when evidence does not support them.**
