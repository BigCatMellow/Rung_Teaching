# The Teaching Loop

Rung uses a repeated eight-stage loop. The teacher should be able to name which stage they are in and why.

```text
ORIENT
  ↓
ATTEMPT
  ↓
DIAGNOSE
  ↓
EXPLAIN
  ↓
MINIMUM HELP
  ↓
REATTEMPT
  ↓
VERIFY
  ↓
TRANSFER
```

A ninth step—**record the lesson**—is used when the correction reveals something reusable.

## 1. Orient

State the skill being practiced and what success looks like.

Do not front-load every relevant fact. Give enough context for a meaningful attempt.

Useful prompt:

> What are we trying to be able to decide or do here?

## 2. Attempt

The learner acts before receiving unnecessary help.

This provides two things:

1. practice doing the reasoning;
2. evidence about where the reasoning fails.

An attempt should be realistic enough to expose the actual skill, not a ceremonial question whose answer was just supplied in the previous sentence.

Research on **productive failure** provides qualified support for problem solving before full instruction in some settings: under appropriate design, generating and comparing imperfect solutions before consolidation can improve conceptual learning and transfer. It does **not** mean unsupported struggle is always useful. See Kapur (2014) on the [Research Foundations](Research-Foundations) page.

## 3. Diagnose

Do not immediately repair the work.

Use one specific question to locate the problem.

```text
question
→ learner answer
→ test the answer
→ next question
```

Avoid stacking several major questions at once. One-at-a-time questioning makes the learner perform each reasoning step and makes the failure point visible.

## 4. Explain

Ask the learner to make their reasoning explicit.

Examples:

- Why does that method apply here?
- What evidence supports that conclusion?
- What would have to change for your answer to change?
- Which standing principle are you using?
- Why would the competing approach fail?

This uses **self-explanation** as a learning mechanism. Research by Chi and colleagues found that learners who generated explanations connecting examples and principles developed stronger understanding, and experimental prompting of self-explanation improved learning. See [Research Foundations](Research-Foundations).

## 5. Minimum help

If the learner is stuck, move only as far up the [Assistance Ladder](Assistance-Ladder) as necessary.

The key question is not:

> Can I give them the answer?

It is:

> What is the smallest intervention that restores productive reasoning?

## 6. Reattempt

The learner—not the teacher—changes the work.

This is important. Hearing a correction and producing a correction are different activities.

If the teacher rewrites the answer, the teacher has demonstrated competence. The learner has not.

## 7. Verify

Check the revised answer against the criterion that originally failed.

Do not accept:

- "seems better";
- "I think it works now";
- confidence alone.

Ask whether the specific diagnostic now passes.

This adapts MAPS's emphasis on observable acceptance criteria and evidence rather than declaring work complete because an implementation looks plausible. See [`TASK_LIFECYCLE.md`](https://github.com/BigCatMellow/MAPS_Lean/blob/main/playbook/TASK_LIFECYCLE.md).

## 8. Transfer

Change the example enough that simple imitation is no longer sufficient.

A useful transfer task changes one or more of:

- surface wording;
- context;
- irrelevant details;
- problem order;
- apparent similarity;
- competing method;
- missing information.

The learner should have to recognize the underlying structure.

## 9. Record the lesson

If the same correction is likely to matter again, capture it.

Use this progression:

```text
OBSERVATION
→ POSSIBLE PATTERN
→ TEST
→ REPEATED EVIDENCE
→ STANDING PRINCIPLE
```

Do not turn every one-off correction into a permanent rule.

MAPS uses a similar principle in its repair and emergence systems: recurring failures deserve durable countermeasures, while useful discoveries should be tested before being promoted into standing machinery. See [`REPAIR_AND_LEARNING.md`](https://github.com/BigCatMellow/MAPS_Lean/blob/main/playbook/REPAIR_AND_LEARNING.md) and [`EMERGENCE.md`](https://github.com/BigCatMellow/MAPS_Lean/blob/main/playbook/EMERGENCE.md).

## Periodic steering check

A locally successful lesson can still be practicing the wrong thing.

At checkpoints ask:

1. Is this still the skill we intended to learn?
2. Is the learner doing more of the reasoning than before?
3. Are exercises attacking the actual weakness?
4. Has a prerequisite gap changed the plan?
5. Are we polishing the project instead of learning the target skill?
6. What evidence would justify moving on?

Then choose:

- **CONTINUE**
- **CHANGE APPROACH**
- **NARROW SCOPE**
- **TEACH PREREQUISITE**
- **RESEARCH**
- **TEST MASTERY**
- **STOP**

This is adapted from MAPS's program-steering and checkpoint discipline. See [`PROGRAM_STEERING.md`](https://github.com/BigCatMellow/MAPS_Lean/blob/main/playbook/PROGRAM_STEERING.md).
